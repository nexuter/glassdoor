01The finding that drives everything else

Before any schema work, one structural question had to be settled: are the monthly directories deltas or full dumps?

Measured, not assumed

Every monthly directory is a full, cumulative re-dump. Comparing the complete 2022-06 and 2022-07 company vintages record by record:

# 202206 vs 202207, both feeds in full, doc payload hashed
                companies              reviews
  202206          829,559             17,096,860
  202207          829,589             17,228,547
  common          829,559             17,096,860
  dropped               0                     0
  added                30                131,687
  doc identical   826,000  99.57%   17,076,493  99.88%
  doc changed       3,559   0.43%       20,367   0.12%

Nothing is ever withdrawn, and the payloads are close to static — four company rows in a thousand change from one month to the next, and barely one review row in a thousand. Stored literally, 47 snapshots would be about 56 million company-rows and 1.4 billion review-rows, more than 99% of them exact duplicates of a row already present.

A trap in the change detection

Hash the whole record rather than just doc and reviews appear to churn at 11.7% a month. That is _meta.updated_at_timestamp moving on every re-scrape, not content changing. Version on the payload, not the record.

So the design is: load each entity once and version only what changes. A type-2 slowly-changing dimension keyed on a row hash collapses companies to ~1.9 M base rows plus a few million change rows, and reviews to the ~41 M reviews that actually exist. "The panel as of 2024-03" stays available — it becomes a range predicate rather than a stored copy.

02What is actually on disk

Two parallel trees, one directory per vintage, gzipped JSON shards three levels down — plus four sidecar files per vintage that are more useful than they look.

Licensing shapes the deployment

Each vintage ships a disclaimer stating the data is subject to copyright and trade-secret rights, and that neither the database structure nor the data may be disclosed publicly or re-distributed. The web interface therefore has to be authenticated and access-logged from day one — this is not polish that can be deferred to the end.

Shard format

Every .json.gz holds exactly 10,000 records, pretty-printed as a JSON array with one complete record per line (leading tab, trailing comma). The loader can stream line by line — no incremental JSON parser, and shards parallelise trivially. Measured on this machine, single-threaded: ~43,500 records/s including gunzip, id extraction and hashing. A full 47-vintage pass is roughly 9 hours serial, 1.5 hours at eight workers.

One trap: the directory bucket is the first three hex characters of the filename, and the filename is md5(first record's doc.id). It is not a content-hash bucket. Chunk boundaries and filenames shift between vintages, so two months cannot be diffed by filename.

03Coverage over time

Growth is stepwise, not organic. Coresignal widened coverage several times; those steps are collection events, not firm entry, and a naive panel would read them as such.

Companies (left axis) Reviews (right axis)

The jumps are large and abrupt. Companies gain +134 k in 2023-07, +231 k in 2023-11, +130 k in 2025-03 and +383 k in 2025-10; reviews gain +5.4 M in 2023-06 and +3.2 M in 2026-03. Any analysis that reads a firm's first appearance as an entry event, or a review-volume jump as a surge in posting, will pick up these collection events instead. The panel needs an explicit first_vintage column, and the codebook has to name these dates.

Consequence for panel work

Any variable introduced after 2023 is missing by construction both for older vintages and for stale records inside new vintages. The normaliser must branch on version_id, not on vintage. The codebook needs a first_available column per variable, and the exporter should attach a coverage note to every download.

06Database design

PostgreSQL as the system of record, with a Parquet mirror as the bulk-export fast path.

Postgres brings concurrent users, authentication, row-level access control, an audit trail, and adequate full-text search over review text via a GIN index on tsvector. A DuckDB instance reading a partitioned Parquet copy of the same tables handles the heavy CSV exports and gives anyone who wants it an offline analysis path. Estimated footprint: about 100 GB in Postgres — reviews with text and indexes account for 60–80 GB of that — and about 35 GB as Parquet with zstd.

ClickHouse would beat both on full-corpus text scans, but it adds operational burden a small research group probably should not take on. Worth revisiting only if text search proves slow in practice.

Reference tables for industry, company_type, employee_count_band, revenue_band and country, so that every filter in the UI is a dropdown rather than a free-text box.

Indexes that matter

  • review (company_sk, review_date) — the dominant access path by a wide margin
  • review (review_date) within each partition
  • review (star_overall), (language), (author_status) — the selective filters
  • GIN on review_text.search_tsv for keyword search across pros / cons / summary / advice
  • Trigram index on company.name for the autocomplete, plus the SCD range index on company_version

07The web interface

The test is that someone who has never written SQL can define a sample, choose variables, and walk away with a CSV. Five screens do that.

01Company finder

Name autocomplete plus filters on industry, country, size band, revenue band, founding year and rating range. Or skip the filters entirely and paste in a list of Glassdoor company ids to define the sample directly.

02Review query builder

Sample × date range × filters — overall and sub-ratings, recommend / CEO approval / business outlook, author status, current vs former employee, tenure, language, has-text — plus keyword search in pros, cons, summary and advice. Variables come from a grouped checkbox list, with a live row-count estimate before you commit.

03Company panel builder

The thing raw Glassdoor cannot give you: choose companies, a date range, a frequency and variables, and get a balanced monthly panel assembled from the version history — with the coverage-break and first-available warnings from §03 and §04 attached to the output.

04Download centre

Every non-trivial request runs as a background job, exactly the WRDS model. CSV, TSV, Parquet or Stata, gzipped, behind expiring links — with the exact query parameters stored alongside each output so a result can be reproduced and cited.

05Codebook

Generated from the schema plus the profiling statistics in §05, so every variable page shows its type, its coverage, how sentinels were handled, and the vintage from which it is available at all.

Alongside those: a read-only SQL console for power users on a separate database role with a statement timeout and a row cap; mandatory login with terms acceptance; and a download audit log, which the licence effectively requires.

For the stack, FastAPI and SQLAlchemy with a JSON-to-SQL query-builder layer, arq or Celery workers for exports, and a server-rendered HTMX front end — fast to build and with no single-page app to maintain — unless a React front end is specifically wanted.

08Phased plan

Roughly eight to eleven weeks for one engineer to a usable internal service. The phases are genuinely sequential — each depends on the one before.

Validation gates

P1 and P2 are not finished until, for every vintage: rows loaded equals wc -l ids_*.txt; every source_id in the manifest resolves; no review lands in a date partition outside 2008-01 to the present; and sentinel counts match the profiling baseline recorded in §05.

09Decisions needed before P1

Six questions when this review started; one of them has since been settled by measurement. The rest change the deployment more than the design.

  1. Reviews — full history, or current state only? Answered: keep the history

    Review payload churn measures 0.12% a month — 20,367 changed rows between 2022-06 and 2022-07. A review_version table therefore costs on the order of one million rows across all 47 vintages, which is nothing. Build it.

  2. Postgres, or DuckDB only?

    How many concurrent users? If this is really one to three people doing research, DuckDB on Parquet with a thin front end roughly halves the build.

  3. Where does it run?

    A local server on this D: volume — 5.5 TB free, comfortably enough — or institutional or cloud hosting? This determines the authentication story.

  4. Who may log in?

    Given the Coresignal terms, presumably named accounts inside the licensed institution only. Worth confirming before P6 rather than after.

  5. Export ceilings.

    Row cap per download, retention period, and whether raw review text may be exported in bulk or only in aggregate.

  6. Do the .zip archives go?

    Deleting them after byte-verifying the extracted trees reclaims about 288 GB.
